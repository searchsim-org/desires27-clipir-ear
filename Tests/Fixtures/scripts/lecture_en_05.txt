Good afternoon, and welcome to the lecture on renewable energy and storage.
Today we look at one community that is trying to run almost entirely on wind and sun.
The pilot project runs on an island in the North Sea, with about three thousand residents.
Being an island makes it a perfect test, because every unit of energy has to be accounted for.
Until recently the island relied on an old diesel generator and an undersea cable to the mainland.
The cable is ageing, and replacing it would cost more than the whole renewable project.
So the residents voted to see how far they could get on their own.
Let us start with generation.
The solar farm on the island produces about forty megawatts at its peak on a clear summer day.
In winter that drops to a small fraction, so solar alone is never enough.
The island also has six wind turbines along the western cliffs.
The turbines are switched off when the wind speed rises above twenty five metres per second.
That protects the blades during storms, which are common in autumn.
On a typical day, wind and solar together cover most of the demand.
The difficult days are calm, grey days in the middle of winter.
Engineers call those periods dark doldrums, and every storage plan is built around them.
Generation is only half the story, the other half is storage.
The main battery uses lithium iron phosphate cells, which are safer and last longer than older chemistries.
It can supply the whole island for about four hours.
For longer gaps, the team experimented with green hydrogen.
Turning electricity into hydrogen and back again loses about a third of the energy along the way.
Even so, worldwide, pumped hydro is still the largest form of energy storage by far.
You pump water uphill when power is cheap and let it run down through turbines when it is needed.
The island has no mountains, so that option was never on the table.
Now to demand, which is where the residents come in.
Most homes replaced their oil boilers with heat pumps over the last five years.
A heat pump moves about three units of heat for every unit of electricity it uses.
That makes electric heating far cheaper than it first appears.
Many residents also charge their cars at night, when the wind is often strongest.
A simple price signal tells their chargers when electricity is plentiful.
Shifting demand like this turned out to be cheaper than adding more batteries.
One more technical detail, because it matters for the grid.
The grid frequency in Europe is fifty hertz, and it must stay very close to that value.
The battery helps here too, because it can respond in a fraction of a second.
