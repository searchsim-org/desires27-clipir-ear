Welcome, everyone, to today's session on coral reefs and why they matter so much.
Reefs look enormous when you swim over them, but they are rare on a global scale.
Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor, yet they shelter about a quarter of all marine species.
That imbalance is exactly why their decline worries biologists.
Let me explain what a coral actually is, because it surprises most students.
A coral is an animal, a colony of tiny polyps that build a skeleton of limestone.
The tiny algae living inside the coral tissue give corals their colour and most of their food.
When the water gets too warm, the coral expels those algae and turns white.
That is what we call bleaching.
A bleached coral is not dead yet, but it is starving, and the clock is ticking.
From a boat, a bleached reef looks almost like snow on the sea bed.
Photographs from the air are now one of the fastest ways to map the damage.
So how warm is too warm? Less than you might think.
Bleaching starts when the water stays about one degree above the usual summer maximum for several weeks.
A single hot summer can bleach a reef that is hundreds of years old.
Some corals recover if the heat passes quickly, others starve and die.
Recovery is possible, and we have watched it happen.
After the last major bleaching event, our study reef recovered in about a decade.
Fast growing branching corals came back first, and the slow massive corals followed later.
But if the hot summers come every few years, there is no time to recover at all.
Now a word about the fish, because reefs are not only coral.
Parrotfish scrape algae off the rock, and in doing so they grind up coral and produce sand.
A large parrotfish can make hundreds of kilograms of sand every year.
Many white beaches in the tropics are, quite literally, made by fish.
Let me describe how we collect our data.
Our dive surveys are done at a depth of fifteen metres, along fixed lines on the reef.
Divers photograph every square metre, and software counts the living coral later.
We also have sensor buoys that send temperature data every thirty minutes.
Tissue samples go back to the lab, where they are stored at minus eighty degrees.
