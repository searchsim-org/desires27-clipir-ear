Hello and welcome to the third lecture on the history of bridge engineering.
Today we follow one river crossing through nine centuries, from stone arches to modern suspension bridges.
Our case study is a crossing over a tidal estuary, which makes every design decision harder.
The water level changes by several metres twice a day, and the currents are strong.
Let us begin with the oldest structure, because parts of it still stand.
The oldest stone bridge still in use in the region was built in the year eleven forty.
It has seven small arches, and each pier sits on a bed of timber piles driven into the mud.
Those timber piles survived because they stayed wet and away from oxygen.
When the river was dredged in the nineteenth century, two of the piers began to settle.
The next big change came with iron and then steel.
Iron allowed longer spans, but early iron bridges were brittle in cold weather.
Steel solved much of that, yet it brought its own problem.
The biggest enemy of steel bridges is corrosion, and most of it is caused by road salt in winter.
Salt water runs into the joints and sits there for months.
Then came the material that changed everything, reinforced concrete.
Concrete is strong when squeezed and weak when stretched, so steel bars carry the tension.
It is cheap, it can be poured into almost any shape, and it does not need painting.
Most of the short bridges you drive over every day are built this way.
Even so, concrete cracks with time, and water in those cracks slowly attacks the steel inside.
Now to the star of the case study, the suspension bridge that opened in the nineteen sixties.
It has a main span of one thousand two hundred metres between the towers.
The two main cables are spun on site from thousands of individual steel wires.
Spinning the cables alone took almost a year.
Long, light decks have a dangerous habit, they can start to oscillate in the wind.
After wind tunnel testing, the engineers added slots along the deck to let the air pass through.
Those slots break up the vortices that would otherwise make the deck swing.
The same idea appears in almost every long span built since.
Keeping a bridge like this alive is a full time job.
Today it carries more than three hundred strain gauges that report how the steel is stretching.
Engineers also climb the cables in person, and a full inspection happens every six years.
Between inspections, the sensor data tells them where to look first.
A few practical points before we stop.

Wear sturdy shoes and bring a warm jacket, it is always windy on the deck.
And the reading for next week is chapter seven, on cable stayed bridges.
